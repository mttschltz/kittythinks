We've examined a quartet of opinions here. There are those who state that it is likely too late (and the end is nigh), those who feel we are all overreacting, and those who can see both sides but advocate for a nuanced approach. And of course, those who think we are asking the wrong question.

For part IV, I will give my opinion, based on the learnt material and personal point of view.

_**Do we need to change our lifestyle (and demands) or is it fearmongering to worry?**_

Neither of the three arguments concerning scientific viewpoints was fully convincing to me.

Both Ehrlich & Ehrlich and Foley are trying to convince us that only unrealistically large changes in our consumption and practices can save us from certain doom. 

But Paul Ehrlich's original predictions didn't come to pass, and he did not publicly and sufficiently acknowledge when he was wrong, which I consider a failure of scientific integrity.\
Furthermore, Ehrlich's insistence on overpopulation being a major issue in food security has simply been shown to be unlikely. Countries with significant increases in wealth have exhibited a _reduction_ in children per woman. And yet, that has not solved the food security problems outright. More affluence (and fewer children) has led to changing diets and more consumption of goods instead. 

Jonathan Foley, too, has flaws in his assertions. While he acknowledges more pressing issues (such as the lack of new farmable land), his idea of 'terraculture' is much too vague and crude to be helpful. How different is it from existing methods of sustainable agriculture ideas, really? Where is the novel approach to actually convincing policy-makers to implement these strategies?

I do believe strongly that _some_ changes are necessary in order to prevent future clashes between climate change, agricultural output and population changes. However, changes must inevitably take into consideration that different regions of the world, and indeed different regions in one country, have different political, social and environmental challenges that can not be solved with a one-size-fits-all approach. Population issues can go both ways. Indeed, some of the richest countries right now (2026) suffer risks due to having an ageing and dying population (leading to a lack of able-bodied workers and a disinterest among the overeducated youth to work in food production at all). Also, agricultural fixes are not necessarily leading to food security if they cannot be distributed and moulded to the current population and their social norms.

The optimists are also questionable. Lomborg in particular is disagreeable. His book is full of cherry-picked data and an almost insidious lack of acknowledgement of the importance of biodiversity.\
Simultaneously, Rosling may have better intentions as a physician, but he too cherry-picks upward-trending data and omits negative trends entirely. His idea that making poorer people richer will not lead to them wanting the same benefits as the currently rich is also delusional.
